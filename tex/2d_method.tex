\chapter{Methodology}

This chapter describes the two segmentation methodologies developed in the present work for the identification and separation of individual glass fibers in three-dimensional micro-CT images. Both methods share a common pre-processing pipeline --- Otsu binarization followed by morphological opening --- and both rely on the skeletonization of the binary fiber mask to reduce the complexity of fiber intersections to a set of discrete junction points, at which the decision of which branches belong to the same continuous fiber must be made. They differ, however, in the space in which segmentation is performed and in the strategy adopted to resolve those junctions.

The first method, described in Section~\ref{method1}, operates in two sequential stages. The three-dimensional volume is sliced into a series of two-dimensional cross-sectional layers, each of which is individually segmented through skeleton-based orientation pruning and watershed labeling; the independently labeled layers are then reconciled into a globally consistent three-dimensional representation through a layer-to-layer label propagation procedure based on spatial overlap between adjacent cross-sections. The second method, described in Section~\ref{method2}, was motivated by an inherent limitation of this two-stage architecture: errors introduced in either the segmentation or the reconstruction stage can compound, reducing the reliability of the final result. To avoid this, the second method performs fiber segmentation directly on the full three-dimensional volume, extending the orientation-based pruning logic to three dimensions through line fitting by Singular Value Decomposition and an iterative branch-pairing strategy capable of handling the more complex junction topologies that arise in the denser three-dimensional skeleton.

\section{2D Segmentation and 3D Reconstruction}
\markright{\thesection ~~~ Methodology1}
\label{method1}

The first approach to the problem was a computationally lightweight methodology, inspired by the work of Rizvandi et al.\ \cite{rizvandi} on the detection of overlapped nematode worms. Despite the apparent difference in application domain, the two problems share a fundamental structural challenge: given a skeleton extracted from a binary image, deciding which segments that meet at a junction point belong to the same continuous object and which ones do not. In both cases, the core difficulty lies in resolving ambiguous intersections between elongated, curvilinear structures, a problem that is geometrically equivalent whether the objects in question are biological organisms or glass fibers embedded in a polymer matrix. 

To detect and separate individual \textit{C.\ elegans} nematode worms in population images containing overlapping specimens, Rizvandi et al.\ \cite{rizvandi} proposed a four-stage pipeline based on skeleton analysis. In the pre-processing stage, the input image is smoothed with a Gaussian kernel and binarized using Otsu's global thresholding method, followed by morphological erosion and hole-filling operations to produce a clean binary mask. A morphological skeletonization is then applied, and spurious short branches resulting from residual noise are pruned by splitting the skeleton at junction pixels (defined as pixels with more than two neighbors) and discarding branches below a minimum length threshold. In the skeleton analysis stage, each junction pixel is removed, decomposing the skeleton into a set of isolated branches. For each connection pixel at the boundary of a branch, a local orientation angle is computed by averaging the successive angular differences across a window of neighboring pixels along that branch. In the angle analysis and tree construction stage, these local angles are used to decide which branches should be joined: starting from each free endpoint, a tree is grown by iteratively appending the neighboring branch whose orientation is most consistent with the current trajectory, accepting the merge only if the angular difference between the two branches falls below a user-defined threshold. This greedy angle-based merging reconstructs the continuous path of each individual worm through the junction points without requiring any prior knowledge of the objects' positions. Finally, a post-processing stage filters out spurious detections by retaining only those reconstructed paths whose length falls within a statistically determined interval, derived from a reference set of isolated individuals.

The methodology proposed in this work will be detailed in the following subsections, but can be summarized in two main stages. In the first stage, the three-dimensional volumetric image is sectioned into a series of two-dimensional layers along the Z axis, and each layer is individually segmented by skeletonizing the binary fiber mask, pruning the skeleton at junction points according to a local orientation criterion, and expanding the resulting labeled skeleton segments back to the full fiber cross-section using the watershed transform. In the second stage, the independently labeled layers are combined into a globally consistent three-dimensional representation by propagating fiber identifiers across consecutive layers on the basis of the spatial overlap between adjacent cross-sections.

\subsection{Slicing Axis Selection and Pre-processing}

Analysing the 3D micro-CT image (Figure~\ref{fig:raw}), the first decision to be made was the choice of slicing axis --- that is, the direction along which the volumetric image would be iteratively sectioned into 2D layers, each of which would be individually segmented before being stacked back to reconstruct a fully segmented 3D representation. A previous attempt by a researcher at the Computational Intelligence Laboratory (LITC) applied the OpenFiberSeg method \cite{sosa-rey} by slicing along the X axis, producing layers in the \textit{ZY} plane in which most fibers appear running parallel to the cross-section (left image in Figure~\ref{fig:sections}). The results obtained with that approach were unsatisfactory, for the reasons discussed in the previous chapter. The present work therefore adopts a different perspective, slicing the volume along the Z axis and producing \textit{XY} layers (right image in Figure~\ref{fig:sections}). This choice is justified by the expectation that most fibers are preferentially aligned with the X axis; consequently, each individual fiber should appear in only a small number of consecutive \textit{XY} layers --- ideally on the order of its mean diameter expressed in pixels. With each fiber spanning fewer layers, the subsequent 3D reconstruction becomes considerably more tractable. The Z axis was preferred over the Y axis as the slicing direction because it yields a smaller total number of layers, thereby reducing the computational cost of the reconstruction step.

In the pre-processing stage, the 3D image was binarized using Otsu's global thresholding method \cite{otsu}, which automatically determines an intensity threshold that maximally separates foreground (fiber) and background voxels. The resulting binary image was then subjected to a morphological opening filter (a sequential erosion followed by a dilation) which smooths the fiber boundaries and suppresses spurious small protrusions introduced by the binarization process. Alternative filters, including median filtering and morphological closing (hole filling), were evaluated but discarded, as they caused artificial thickening of the fiber cross-sections and consequently increased the degree of apparent overlap between adjacent fibers.

With the filtered binary image in hand, a morphological skeletonization was applied \textcolor{red}{(REF IMAGEM DO ESQUELETO)}, reducing each fiber cross-section to a single-pixel-wide medial axis. Skeletonization is a critical pre-processing step in this pipeline: if the full binary image were used directly, regions where two or more fibers overlap would appear as extended multi-pixel blobs, making the subsequent disambiguation between touching fibers considerably more difficult. By contrast, the unifilar skeleton collapses each such overlapping region into a single pixel or a very small cluster of pixels, greatly simplifying the structural analysis of fiber junctions.

\subsection{2D Angle Analysis and Pruning}

The pruning stage constitutes the core of the 2D segmentation step, in which the algorithm decides, at each junction point of the skeleton, which pairs of converging branches belong to the same continuous fiber and which ones originate from distinct fibers crossing one another. To formalize this decision process, it is necessary to first define the structural elements of the skeleton.

A \textbf{junction pixel} is any skeleton pixel that has three or more immediate neighbors also belonging to the skeleton, indicating a point where multiple fiber paths converge. An \textbf{endpoint pixel} is a skeleton pixel with exactly one neighbor, marking the terminal extremity of a fiber or branch. A \textbf{branch} is then defined as a connected chain of skeleton pixels linking two such special pixels. Three branch types are distinguished: a \textit{JP--JP branch} connects two junction pixels; a \textit{JP--EP branch} connects a junction pixel to an endpoint; and an \textit{EP--EP branch} connects two endpoints, corresponding to an isolated fiber segment that does not intersect any other structure.

Once all branches have been mapped, a filtering step is applied in which each branch is assessed according to its type and its length, measured as the number of pixels composing the branch. Branches of type EP--EP whose length falls below a minimum threshold of 5 pixels are removed entirely, as they are considered to be artefacts of the skeletonization or residual noise rather than meaningful fiber segments. JP--EP branches below the same threshold are also removed, with the exception of the pixel at the junction end, which is preserved so as not to disrupt the topological connectivity of the junction with its remaining branches. JP--JP branches, however, are not filtered by length, since removing short inter-junction segments could erroneously sever a genuine fiber at a location where two junction points happen to lie in close proximity along its axis.

The outcome of this filtering step is a cleaner set of 2D skeleton images with fewer spurious fragments, which facilitates the subsequent decision-making at junction points. The segmentation logic then iterates over all junction pixels in each layer. At each junction pixel, the local orientation angle of every incident branch with respect to the Y axis is computed. To ensure that this angle accurately reflects the fiber's trajectory in the immediate vicinity of the junction, rather than its overall direction across the full branch length, the angle is calculated using a fixed local window: for branches longer than 12 pixels, the angle is determined by the line connecting the junction pixel to the 12th pixel along the branch; for shorter branches, the endpoint is used instead. This local windowing strategy was adopted after observing that computing the angle from the junction point to the branch endpoint produced unreliable results for curved fibers, as the global direction of a long curved branch can diverge significantly from its actual local orientation near the junction, leading to erroneous collinearity decisions, and its threshold of 12 pixels was chosen empirically. These locally computed angles are then compared pairwise across all branches meeting at the junction: the pair whose angles sum to a value closest to 180° are considered collinear and assigned to the same fiber, provided that their sum is no less than 45°. This lower bound was chosen empirically and acts as a sharpness filter, a junction angle below 45° would imply an abrupt change in fiber direction that is physically incompatible with the behavior of a rigid glass fiber, and is therefore interpreted as evidence of two distinct fibers crossing rather than a single fiber continuing through the junction. The remaining branches at that junction are interpreted as belonging to distinct fibers and are pruned by setting their first pixel beyond the junction point to zero, effectively disconnecting them from the junction and separating the crossing fiber paths.

This decision criterion relies on the assumption that fibers follow locally linear trajectories in the vicinity of each junction point. This assumption is physically grounded in the nature of the material system and its manufacturing process. Since the composite samples analyzed in this work were produced by thermoplastic injection molding \cite{aguiar-base}, the fibers are expected to behave as rigid rods throughout processing, as described by the Folgar--Tucker model \cite{folgar1984}. Under the high shear rates characteristic of injection molding, short glass fibers align preferentially along the flow direction and exhibit locally linear trajectories, a behavior that has been experimentally confirmed for this material system through micro-CT fiber orientation measurements \cite{aguiar-base}.

\subsection{Fiber Labeling}

After pruning the skeleton at each junction point, the resulting disconnected branches can be individually labeled, with each label corresponding to a distinct fiber skeleton segment. Ideally, each physical fiber will produce exactly one branch per layer, which is consistent with the nature of glass fibers: since they do not bifurcate or split during the injection molding process, no branching topology is expected in their skeletal representation.

The individual branches are identified and labeled using connected component analysis, a standard image processing operation that assigns a unique integer label to each set of pixels that are spatially connected to one another and disconnected from all others. Following the pruning step, each connected component in the skeleton corresponds to a single branch, and its pixels are assigned a common label value that serves as its identifier throughout the subsequent processing stages.

With all skeleton branches properly labeled, it is necessary to propagate those labels to the full binary fiber mask, so that every foreground pixel in the binary image is associated with a specific fiber identity rather than merely with the fiber class as a whole. This propagation is performed using the watershed transform, a morphological algorithm that treats the grayscale image as a topographic surface and floods it from a set of seed markers. In this context, the labeled skeleton pixels serve as the seed markers, and the watershed algorithm assigns each binary foreground pixel the label of its nearest skeleton branch, effectively partitioning the binary fiber regions according to their proximity to each skeletal segment. The result is a fully labeled 2D binary image in which every fiber pixel carries the identifier of the fiber branch it belongs to.

\subsection{3D Reconstruction}

Although the pruning and labeling steps described above operate on individual 2D layers, each physical fiber is a three-dimensional object that spans multiple consecutive cross-sections. Since each layer is processed independently, there is no guarantee that the same fiber receives the same label across adjacent layers. The 3D reconstruction step addresses this problem: it establishes correspondences between labeled fiber segments across consecutive layers, propagating and unifying labels so that all cross-sections belonging to the same physical fiber share a common identifier.

Before adopting this layer-to-layer matching strategy, an alternative approach was considered: computing the length and orientation of each 2D labeled branch directly from the cross-sectional plane and using these measurements as proxies for the true 3D fiber geometry. This approach was discarded for a fundamental reason. A fiber appears in multiple consecutive layers not only because of its three-dimensional extent in space, but also because of its angular relationship with the slicing plane. A fiber that runs obliquely with respect to the \textit{XY} slicing plane will intersect a larger number of layers than one running perpendicular to it, and its cross-sectional projection will appear as an elongated elliptical region rather than a circular one. Consequently, the orientation and length derived from any single 2D cross-section would not be representative of the true 3D fiber geometry, and aggregating these measurements across layers without first establishing fiber identity would introduce systematic errors in both length and orientation estimates. The 3D reconstruction step is therefore a necessary precondition for any meaningful metric computation.

The reconstruction is carried out by a sequential label propagation procedure that processes consecutive layers in order. The first layer is assigned a set of globally unique integer identifiers, with each connected component receiving a distinct value. For every subsequent layer, each labeled fiber region is compared against the immediately preceding layer by examining the spatial coincidence between the two: for each region in the current layer, the number of pixels it shares with each labeled region in the previous layer is counted, and the previous region with the highest coincidence count is identified as the most likely predecessor.

The match is accepted only if two conditions are simultaneously satisfied: the absolute number of coincident pixels must exceed a minimum threshold, and the coincidence must represent a sufficient fraction of the smaller of the two regions \textcolor{red}{(justificar thresholds)}. This second condition prevents a large region from claiming a small one on the basis of a negligible strip of overlap. When both conditions are met, the current region inherits the identifier of its predecessor, linking it to the same physical fiber tracked in the previous layer. When neither a predecessor nor a sufficiently large overlap is found, a new globally unique identifier is assigned. This situation arises naturally for fibers whose cross-sections first become visible at an intermediate layer, as occurs for obliquely oriented fibers entering or exiting the scanned volume.

When two or more regions in the current layer compete to inherit the identifier of the same predecessor, the conflict is resolved by assigning that identifier to the region with the greater absolute overlap, while each of the remaining regions receives a new identifier. This resolution strategy is consistent with the assumption that a single physical fiber occupies a spatially connected and non-branching region across consecutive layers, an assumption that is well supported by the rigid-rod nature of the glass fibers present in the material.

The outcome of the propagation procedure is a fully labeled three-dimensional volume in which every foreground voxel is associated with a globally unique identifier corresponding to a single physical fiber. This labeled volume is then used as the basis for all subsequent fiber-level measurements, including length, orientation, and spatial distribution.

\section{3D segmentation}
\markright{\thesection ~~~ Metodologia2}
\label{method2}

After some initial testing, reconstructing the 3D fibers after doing a 2D segmentation demonstrated to be a complex method. As it unites two complex tasks, it open margins to error propagation: if one fiber is mistakenly segmented, this error will be propagated on the reconstruction stage, and if a fiber is correctly segmented, if an error occurs in the reconstruction stage, it will be wrongly segmented on the 3D space. Considering this complexity, a method that segment the fibers directly on the 3D space in one clear task, highlights.

\subsection{Pre-processing}

This method analyses the hole 3D image, so it is not necessary to choose an axis as on the 2D segmentation. On the pre-processing stage, the binarization and filtering process were the same of the 2D segmentation, Otsu's global thresholding for binarization and a morphological opening filter for noise reduction.

\subsection{3D angle Analysis and Pruning}

The core principles of the decision making follows the same logic of the 2D segmentation, based on the Folgar–Tucker rigid rods model that shows the linear tendency of the glass fibers. The changes in relation to the 2D segmentation are adaptations for the 3D space.

The angles are calculated fitting a 3D line to the voxels of each branch that touch a junction point using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), a method that minimizes the perpendicular distances from the voxels to the line. The pruning decision used the same logic of branches with angles summing closest to 180° being kept together. However, on the 3D space, each voxel has 26 possible neighbours, while on the 2D plane, each pixel had only 8. This increase of complexity means that on the higher space, there is more chance of a voxel being a junction point and it having much more than 3 or 4 neighbours. With that in mind, it was necessary an adjustment on the pruning methodology: instead of calculating the angles and keeping only the two branches with more linear behaviour, the method iteratively finds valid pairs of branches.

\section{3D Segmentation}
\label{method2}

After initial testing, the two-stage approach of the previous method (performing 2D segmentation layer by layer and subsequently reconstructing the 3D fiber identities) revealed a fundamental structural weakness: the pipeline concatenates two independently complex tasks, and errors introduced in either stage compound in the final result. A fiber incorrectly segmented in the 2D pruning step will carry that error into the reconstruction, and a fiber correctly segmented in 2D may still be wrongly reconstructed in 3D if the layer-to-layer matching fails. This error propagation, inherent to any multi-stage pipeline, motivates the development of a method that operates directly on the full 3D volume, treating segmentation as a single unified task rather than a sequence of dependent sub-problems.

\subsection{Pre-processing}

Since this method operates on the full 3D image at once, no slicing axis selection is required. The pre-processing pipeline mirrors that of the 2D method: the volume is binarized using Otsu's global thresholding \cite{otsu}, and the resulting binary image is smoothed with a morphological opening filter to suppress spurious small protrusions introduced by the binarization process. The median filtering and morphological closing filters that were discarded in the 2D case were equally discarded here for the same reason: they cause artificial thickening of fiber cross-sections and increase the degree of apparent overlap between adjacent fibers.

Following binarization and filtering, a 3D morphological skeletonization is applied, reducing each fiber to a single-voxel-wide medial axis. As in the 2D case, skeletonization is a critical step: it collapses regions of fiber overlap into a minimal set of junction voxels, making the subsequent disambiguation between crossing fibers tractable. The resulting 3D skeleton is then subjected to the same branch filtering procedure described in the previous method, in which short spurious branches are identified and removed. Specifically, JP--EP branches shorter than 5 voxels and EP--EP branches shorter than 15 voxels are deleted, with junction voxels preserved to maintain the topological structure of the skeleton \textcolor{red}{(verificar os valores exatos de threshold usados no código 3D e confirmar se são iguais aos do método 2D)}.

\subsection{3D Angle Analysis and Pruning}

The core logic of the pruning step follows the same physical rationale as the 2D method: under the rigid rod assumption formalized by the Folgar--Tucker model \cite{folgar1984}, glass fibers processed by injection molding are expected to follow locally linear trajectories \cite{aguiar-base}, and junction points in the skeleton where two or more fibers cross can therefore be resolved by identifying which pairs of incident branches are most collinear.

The primary adaptation required for the 3D setting concerns the computation of branch orientation vectors. In the 2D method, the local orientation of a branch at a junction was represented as a scalar angle with respect to the Y axis, computed from the direction of a line segment. In 3D, orientation is instead represented as a unit vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$. To compute this vector robustly and to avoid the staircase artefact introduced by the discrete voxel grid, in which diagonal directions are approximated by step-wise sequences of axis-aligned voxels, the direction of each branch is estimated by fitting a 3D line to the first  12 voxels (or the endpoint in case of branches smaller than 12 voxels, as on the 2D segmentation) of the branch starting from the junction point, using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD). SVD finds the principal axis of the voxel cloud that minimizes the sum of squared perpendicular distances from the voxels to the line, yielding a smooth and robust estimate of the local fiber direction. The resulting vector is then oriented to point away from the junction, ensuring a consistent directional convention across all branches \textcolor{red}{(confirmar se o local\_range padrão de 10 voxels é o mesmo usado nos experimentos, ou se foi ajustado)}.

The collinearity criterion is also adapted for 3D. In the 2D method, two branches were considered collinear if their scalar angles summed to a value closest to 180°. In 3D, the equivalent condition is that the two unit direction vectors point in approximately opposite directions, that is, their dot product is close to $-1$, corresponding to an angle between them close to 180°. Rather than selecting only the single most collinear pair at each junction, the 3D method employs an iterative pairing strategy to correctly handle complex junctions where multiple fibers cross simultaneously, such as X-shaped intersections. The algorithm proceeds as follows: at each junction, all pairwise angles between the direction vectors of the incident branches are computed. The pair with the angle closest to 180° is identified; if this angle meets a minimum threshold of \textcolor{red}{(verificar e justificar o valor de min\_angle usado, que no código padrão é 75°, diferente dos 45° do método 2D --- esta diferença é intencional?)}, the pair is accepted as a valid continuous fiber and both branches are removed from the pool of unpaired candidates. This process repeats iteratively on the remaining unpaired branches until no valid pair can be found or fewer than two branches remain. Any branches that fail to find a valid partner are pruned.

The pruning mechanism also differs slightly from the 2D implementation. In the 2D method, a single pixel at the junction end of each pruned branch was set to zero, which was sufficient to break the topological connection in the planar skeleton. In 3D, however, junction regions are spatially more complex: each voxel has up to 26 possible neighbors, and a single-voxel deletion may be insufficient to fully disconnect a branch from the junction volume. To address this, the 3D pruning step removes a fixed number of voxels from the junction end of each pruned branch, pulling the stub away from the junction and ensuring a clean topological disconnection \textcolor{red}{(verificar e justificar o valor de retraction\_dist usado nos experimentos e explicar se foi escolhido empiricamente)}.

\subsection{Fiber Labeling}

Following the pruning step, the branches of the 3D skeleton are individually labeled using connected component analysis, assigning a unique integer identifier to each spatially connected set of voxels. As in the 2D method, each connected component corresponds to one fiber skeleton, and its voxels are assigned a common label. These labels are then propagated to the full binary fiber mask using the 3D watershed transform, which assigns each foreground voxel the label of its nearest skeleton branch, partitioning the binary volume into a set of labeled fiber regions. The result is a fully segmented and labeled 3D fiber map in which every voxel carries the identifier of the fiber it belongs to.
